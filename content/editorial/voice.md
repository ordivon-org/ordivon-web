# Voice and Style

## Voice

Ordivon should sound like an independent technical researcher who has built the system, tested the boundary, changed their mind when evidence required it, and remains willing to state a strong conclusion.

The voice is:

- **assertive**, not inflated;
- **precise**, not bureaucratic;
- **technical**, not needlessly internal;
- **literary when it clarifies**, not decorative;
- **future-facing**, not detached from present evidence;
- **critical**, not permanently defensive;
- **independent**, not company-simulating.

## Core writing rule

> Give the reader the consequence before asking them to learn the architecture.

Preferred sequence:

1. concrete event or tension;
2. why it matters;
3. Ordivon's judgment;
4. evidence and mechanism;
5. formal terminology;
6. limitation and next test.

## Sentence construction

### Prefer concrete subjects and verbs

Weak:

> Completion adjudication remained under Host ownership through provider replacement.

Stronger:

> Host rejected the stale completion after the provider changed.

Then introduce the formal rule:

> This is why completion adjudication remains a Host responsibility.

### Use abstract nouns only when they compress known mechanisms

Words such as continuity, authority, commitment, recovery, evidence, and judgment are central to Ordivon. They become vague when several appear before any actor, event, or consequence.

Avoid:

> Durable semantic continuity preserves recoverable trajectory truth.

Prefer:

> The next model could see what had been accepted, what remained unresolved, and which execution evidence already existed.

### Control density

Use one high-density technical sentence only after two or three plain sentences have established the problem. Do not make every sentence carry a thesis, definition, contrast, and caveat.

### Vary rhythm

A useful paragraph often contains:

- one short orientation sentence;
- one or two explanatory sentences;
- one precise consequence;
- optionally, one memorable closing line.

Strong lines lose force when every paragraph ends like a manifesto.

## Aphorisms and literary language

Literary force is welcome when it performs conceptual work.

Good uses:

- compressing a central contradiction;
- returning to an image established at the opening;
- marking the transition from evidence to judgment;
- giving a conclusion a memorable form.

Examples already worth preserving:

- “Work should survive the intelligence that started it.”
- “Evidence may revise the map; the map may not revise the evidence.”
- “Winning the move can lose the contest.”

Limits:

- normally one major aphoristic sentence above the fold;
- normally no more than one blockquote or thesis callout per major section;
- never use metaphor to hide an undefined mechanism;
- do not replace measured uncertainty with dramatic certainty.

## Affirmative before defensive

Current Ordivon copy often defines itself through negation: not a product diagram, not a second database, no shared fiction, not a delivery forecast.

Negation is useful after a positive identity has been established.

Prefer:

> Four state owners keep one trajectory recoverable. The diagram is a public view of those boundaries, not another source of truth.

Over:

> Ordivon is not a product diagram and not a second knowledge database.

The reader should first learn what exists, then which tempting mistake it avoids.

## Jargon ladder

Introduce terms in this order:

1. plain-language consequence;
2. descriptive phrase;
3. formal Ordivon term;
4. precise type or field when necessary.

Example:

> The external service may have committed even though its response was lost. The system therefore preserves unresolved effect state. Ordivon Runtime represents that condition as `UNKNOWN` and requires reconciliation before redispatch.

Do not begin a homepage card with `UNKNOWN`, `CompletionProposal`, `Assignment generation`, or `TaskOutcome` unless the card is explicitly for an expert research audience.

## Capitalization

Capitalize names of Ordivon projects and formal schema objects only when referring to those exact objects.

Capitalize:

- Ordivon Host, Ordivon Runtime, Ordivon World, Ordivon Computing;
- Host, Runtime, World when used as those named state owners;
- Task, Assignment, Effect, Observation, Artifact, HarnessRunReceipt when referring to exact model objects;
- Codex App Server, Hermes ACP, Station Zero.

Lowercase in ordinary prose:

- agent, model, provider, session, task, process, tool, run, artifact, evidence;
- host, runtime, world, harness when used generically.

Avoid capitalizing generic words merely because they are important. Excess capitalization makes public prose read like a specification.

## Titles

A strong title should normally contain one of:

- a conflict: “Winning the Move Can Lose the Contest”;
- a concrete result: “One Authority, Thirteen Tables Deleted”;
- a corrective judgment: “A Transcript Is Not a Task Database”;
- a practical question: “Why Ordivon Needs a Harness—but Not a Universal Harness”;
- a capability: “How One Task Survived Two Provider Replacements”;
- a large thesis: “The Future Will Not Wait.”

Prefer the public result over internal phase codes. Put H1–H5, R3, M2.1, or version identifiers in the deck, kicker, or metadata unless the identifier itself is the release.

## Decks and descriptions

### Deck

The deck is the reading contract. It should state the problem, result, or central claim in one or two sentences. It may use formal terms after the title gives a clear public frame.

### Description

The description should work independently in search, RSS, cards, and social previews. Avoid lists of internal objects. State:

- what was tested or argued;
- the most important result;
- why it matters.

## Calls to action

A call to action should describe the value of the destination.

Prefer:

- Read the experiment and results
- See both replacement runs
- Inspect the project boundary
- Open the release record
- Start with the architecture guide
- View the source and receipts

Use sparingly:

- Explore
- Learn more
- Open object
- Inspect the graph

“Explore” is acceptable for a broad index. It is weak when a more concrete promise exists.

## Numbers

Use numbers when they change interpretation, not merely because they exist.

Strong:

> Both replacement orders completed; three injected faults were handled; final provider text was not required for accepted completion.

Weak:

> 56 relations and 39 nodes connect 11 articles.

Always provide denominator, scope, unit, and comparison where they matter.

## Before-and-after examples

### Homepage category clarity

Current direction:

> Ordivon preserves Tasks, committed effects, external interactions, and evidence across model sessions, process restarts, machines, and providers.

Editorial direction:

> Ordivon keeps real agent work recoverable when sessions end, processes restart, machines change, or one provider replaces another. It preserves the task decisions and execution evidence a new agent needs to continue safely.

### System page

Current direction:

> Ordivon has a small public model and richer source repositories.

Editorial direction:

> Four state owners keep task meaning, local execution, external interaction, and research contracts from overwriting one another.

The repository/source-of-truth qualification can follow.

### Writing page

Current direction:

> Dated arguments, not a second fact database.

Editorial direction:

> Research reports, architecture decisions, and essays from systems tested through real work.

The fact-database boundary can appear in the supporting paragraph.

### Now page

Current direction:

> The frontier is what could still change the map.

Editorial direction:

> What changed, what we learned, and what we are testing now.

## Review checklist

Before merging public copy:

- Can a new reader identify the subject before the first formal term?
- Does the copy state why the result matters?
- Is the central claim specific enough to disagree with?
- Are observations, inferences, decisions, and forecasts distinguishable?
- Does every number include useful context?
- Is there more affirmative explanation than defensive negation?
- Is one sentence carrying too many abstract nouns?
- Is capitalization reserved for formal objects?
- Does the CTA describe what the reader will obtain?
- Is the strongest line supported by the evidence around it?
